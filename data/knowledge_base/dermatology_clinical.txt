=== ECZEMA (ATOPIC DERMATITIS) ===
Chronic inflammatory skin condition; affects 10–20% of children, 1–3% of adults
Associated with asthma and allergic rhinitis (atopic triad)

Symptoms: Dry, intensely itchy, red/inflamed skin; vesicles that ooze and crust; chronic: lichenified (thickened) plaques
Distribution: Infants — face, scalp, extensor surfaces; children/adults — flexural creases (antecubital, popliteal fossae)

Triggers: Soap/detergents, synthetic fabrics, sweat, stress, food allergens (eggs, milk, nuts in young children), aeroallergens (dust mites, pet dander)

Management:
1. Emollients (moisturisers): Most important step — apply within 3 min of bathing (seal moisture); thick creams better than lotions; apply liberally and frequently
2. Topical corticosteroids: Step up during flares; use appropriate potency for area (mild for face, moderate-strong for body); apply once or twice daily; finger-tip unit guides dosing
3. Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus): Steroid-sparing; safe for face and sensitive areas; especially for maintenance
4. Wet wraps: For severe flares — apply emollient/TCS then wet bandage over dry outer layer
5. Antihistamines: Sedating (promethazine) for sleep only; non-sedating antihistamines not effective for itch
6. Phototherapy: Narrowband UVB — effective for moderate-severe chronic eczema
7. Systemic: Dupilumab (biologic — IL-4/IL-13 inhibitor) — most effective for moderate-severe; excellent safety
8. Infection: Eczema herpeticum (HSV superinfection) — medical emergency; eczema skin colonised with Staph aureus — mupirocin/dilute bleach baths

=== PSORIASIS ===
Chronic immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease; affects 2–3% of population

Types:
- Plaque psoriasis (80%): Well-demarcated, silvery-scaled, erythematous plaques; scalp, elbows, knees, lower back, umbilicus
- Guttate psoriasis: Small teardrop-shaped lesions; triggered by streptococcal throat infection
- Inverse/flexural: Smooth red plaques in skin folds
- Pustular: Generalised or localised white pustules (sterile); may be triggered by stopping steroids
- Erythrodermic: Generalised redness covering >90% body surface; medical emergency

Triggers: Stress, trauma (Koebner phenomenon), throat infection, medications (lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials), smoking, alcohol

Management:
Mild: Topical corticosteroids, Vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol), coal tar
Moderate: Phototherapy (PUVA or NB-UVB)
Severe/Moderate: Methotrexate, ciclosporin, acitretin
Biologics: TNF-alpha inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept), IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab), IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab) — highly effective for moderate-severe

Psoriatic arthritis: Occurs in 30% of psoriasis patients; treat with DMARDs or biologics; NSAIDs for mild joint disease

=== ACNE VULGARIS ===
Chronic pilosebaceous follicle disorder; affects 85% of adolescents; continues into adulthood in 40%

Pathophysiology: Sebum overproduction + follicular hyperkeratinisation + Cutibacterium acnes proliferation + inflammation

Severity:
- Mild: Comedones (blackheads, whiteheads) ± few inflammatory papules
- Moderate: Multiple inflammatory papules and pustules
- Severe: Nodules, cysts, risk of scarring

Treatment (step-up):
Step 1: Topical retinoid (tretinoin, adapalene) alone or + benzoyl peroxide for mild
Step 2: Add topical antibiotic (clindamycin) — always combine with BP to prevent resistance
Step 3: Oral antibiotics (doxycycline or lymecycline) for moderate — 12-week course; combine with topical retinoid
Step 4: Oral isotretinoin (Accutane/Roaccutane) for severe/scarring/treatment-resistant
  - Highly effective (single course 70% achieve remission)
  - Monitoring: LFTs, lipids monthly; strict teratogenicity (2 forms of contraception in women)
  - Side effects: Dryness (lips, skin, eyes), initially worse acne, mood changes (controversial)
Step 5 (women): Combined OCP (ethinylestradiol + cyproterone, drospirenone) — anti-androgenic effect

Skincare advice:
- Wash face twice daily with gentle pH-balanced cleanser; no scrubbing
- Non-comedogenic moisturiser and sunscreen
- Avoid picking/squeezing — increases scarring and infection
- Dietary evidence: High glycaemic index foods and dairy may worsen acne (modest evidence)

=== SKIN CANCER ===

Melanoma:
- Most dangerous skin cancer; arises from melanocytes
- ABCDE rule: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variation, Diameter >6mm, Evolution (change)
- Risk factors: UV exposure, fair skin, multiple naevi (moles), family history, tanning beds
- Staging determines prognosis; thin melanoma (<1mm Breslow thickness) — excellent prognosis with wide local excision
- Treatment: Wide excision; sentinel lymph node biopsy; immunotherapy (pembrolizumab, ipilimumab) for advanced

Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC):
- Most common skin cancer; locally invasive, rarely metastasises
- Pearly/translucent papule with rolled border, telangiectasia, often on face/head
- Treatment: Surgical excision, Mohs surgery (face), radiotherapy, imiquimod cream (superficial BCC)

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC):
- Second most common; can metastasise (especially lip, ear, immunocompromised)
- Crusted, indurated papule or plaque; often on sun-exposed areas
- Actinic keratoses: precursor lesion — treat with cryotherapy, 5-FU cream, photodynamic therapy

Sun protection (primary prevention):
- SPF 30+ broad-spectrum sunscreen (UVA + UVB); reapply every 2h when outdoors
- Protective clothing, wide-brimmed hat
- Avoid sun 10am–4pm when UV index highest
- No tanning beds (increase melanoma risk by 75% if used before age 35)

=== COMMON RASHES — QUICK REFERENCE ===

Non-blanching rash + fever: MENINGOCOCCAL DISEASE — emergency (call 999/112) until proven otherwise
  Glass test: Press glass against rash; if does not fade → non-blanching → meningococcal septicaemia

Chickenpox (varicella): Itchy crops of vesicles at different stages; highly contagious; calamine, antihistamines for itch; aciclovir for immunocompromised or severe

Shingles (herpes zoster): Dermatomal (follows nerve distribution); painful prodrome before rash; treat with aciclovir within 72h; post-herpetic neuralgia may persist months

Urticaria (hives): Raised, itchy, blanchable welts; transient (hours); antihistamines; if angioedema or breathing difficulty → anaphylaxis protocol
Chronic spontaneous urticaria: >6 weeks; non-sedating antihistamines 2nd-gen; specialist referral

Cellulitis: Spreading red, warm, tender skin infection; usually unilateral; treat with antibiotics (flucloxacillin, co-amoxiclav); mark border to monitor spread; hospitalise if systemically unwell

Contact dermatitis: Allergic (delayed hypersensitivity — nickel, latex, fragrance) or irritant (soap, detergents); patch testing for allergen; avoidance, topical steroids
