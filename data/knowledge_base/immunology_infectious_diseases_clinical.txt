=== 1. ACUTE RESPIRATORY VIRAL INFECTIONS: DIFFERENTIAL MATRIX ===
Viral respiratory illnesses share overlapping symptomatology but differ significantly in onset kinetics, complication profiles, and antiviral eligibility windows.

Differential Diagnostic Characteristics:
1. Common Cold (Rhinovirus, Coronaviruses, Adenovirus):
   - Onset: Gradual over 2–3 days.
   - Primary features: Sneezing, clear rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, mild scratchy sore throat.
   - Fever: Rare in adults; mild in young children.
   - Constitutional symptoms: Mild fatigue; body aches minimal or absent.
   - Course: Self-limiting within 7–10 days.

2. Influenza (Influenza A & B):
   - Onset: Sudden, abrupt "hit by a truck" within hours.
   - Primary features: High fever (38.5–40°C / 101–104°F), shaking chills, profound myalgias/arthralgias, severe retro-orbital headache, dry non-productive cough, extreme exhaustion.
   - Antiviral window: Neuraminidase inhibitors (Oseltamivir / Tamiflu 75 mg BID x 5 days) or Cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor (Baloxavir marboxil single dose) are most effective when initiated within 48 hours of symptom onset. Indicated beyond 48 hours in hospitalized, elderly, or severely immunocompromised patients.

3. COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2):
   - Onset: Variable, typically 2–5 days post-exposure.
   - Primary features: Fever, dry or productive cough, shortness of breath, profound fatigue, sore throat, congestion, GI symptoms (nausea, diarrhea ~20%), anosmia/ageusia (loss of smell/taste).
   - High-Risk Management: Oral antivirals (Nirmatrelvir/Ritonavir / Paxlovid) initiated within 5 days of symptom onset in individuals ≥ 50 years or with underlying comorbidities (diabetes, obesity, CKD, immunosuppression). Note: Paxlovid has significant CYP3A drug-drug interactions (statins, antiarrhythmics, direct oral anticoagulants).

4. Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV):
   - Primary features: Copious rhinorrhea, wheezing, tachypnea, bronchiolitis presentation.
   - High risk in infants (< 12 months) and older adults (≥ 60 years) with underlying cardiopulmonary disease (COPD, Congestive Heart Failure).

=== 2. URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS (UTIs) ===
Pathophysiology: Ascending bacterial colonization from periurethral area, primarily uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC; 75-90%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus.

A. Uncomplicated Lower UTI (Acute Cystitis):
- Clinical Features: Dysuria (burning during micturition), urinary frequency, urinary urgency, suprapubic pain, cloudy foul-smelling urine, microscopic or gross hematuria.
- Absence of systemic symptoms: No fever, no flank pain, no rigors, no altered mental status.
- First-Line Empirical Antibiotic Regimens:
  * Nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals: 100 mg orally BID for 5 days (contraindicated if eGFR < 30 mL/min).
  * Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX / Bactrim DS): 1 tablet (160/800 mg) BID for 3 days (avoid if local resistance > 20%).
  * Fosfomycin trometamol: 3 g single oral sachet in water.
- Symptomatic Relief: Phenazopyridine 200 mg TID for max 2 days (warn patient about orange-red discoloration of urine/tears).

B. Complicated Upper UTI (Acute Pyelonephritis) — RED FLAG:
- Clinical Features: Systemic toxicity with high fever (> 38.3°C / 101°F), shaking rigors, flank pain, severe costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness, nausea, vomiting.
- Risk of urosepsis: Requires immediate urine culture, complete blood count, blood cultures if septic, and empirical IV or oral fluoroquinolone (Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID x 7 days) or IV Ceftriaxone (1 g daily).

=== 3. SEPSIS RECOGNITION & EMERGENCY PROTOCOL ===
Definition: Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Mortality increases by 7.6% for every hour antibiotics are delayed in septic shock.

quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) Criteria (Any 2 of 3 indicates high mortality risk):
1. Respiratory Rate: ≥ 22 breaths per minute (Tachypnea)
2. Altered Mental Status: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) < 15 (Confusion, agitation, lethargy, encephalopathy)
3. Systolic Blood Pressure: ≤ 100 mmHg (Hypotension)

Systemic Signs of Sepsis:
- Fever (> 38.3°C) OR Hypothermia (< 36.0°C; hypothermia carries worse prognosis)
- Tachycardia (Heart rate > 90 bpm)
- Decreased urine output (Oliguria < 0.5 mL/kg/h)
- Mottled, cool extremities, delayed capillary refill (> 3 seconds)
- Elevated serum lactate (> 2.0 mmol/L indicates tissue hypoperfusion).

Emergency Sepsis Action:
Dial 911 immediately. Emphasize "Suspected Sepsis" to emergency dispatch. Prompt resuscitation follows the "Surviving Sepsis Campaign Hour-1 Bundle":
- Measure blood lactate level
- Obtain blood cultures prior to administering broad-spectrum antibiotics
- Administer rapid IV broad-spectrum antimicrobials
- Rapid administration of 30 mL/kg crystalloid IV fluid for hypotension or lactate ≥ 4 mmol/L.

=== 4. ADULT IMMUNIZATION & VACCINE SCHEDULES ===
Evidence-Based Recommendations (CDC / ACIP Standards):
1. Influenza (Annual): All individuals ≥ 6 months. High-dose inactivated (Fluzone High-Dose) or adjuvanted vaccine preferred for adults ≥ 65 years.
2. Pneumococcal Conjugate (PCV20 or PCV15 followed by PPSV23): All adults ≥ 65 years, or adults 19-64 with risk conditions (chronic lung/heart/liver disease, diabetes, smoking, immunocompromised status).
3. Herpes Zoster (Shingles — Shingrix): Two-dose recombinant zoster vaccine for all adults ≥ 50 years (given 2 to 6 months apart), regardless of prior history of chickenpox or live shingles vaccine. > 90% efficacy in preventing shingles and postherpetic neuralgia.
4. Tdap (Tetanus, Diphtheria, Pertussis): Once every 10 years booster for all adults; 1 dose during each pregnancy (27–36 weeks gestation) to confer neonatal pertussis immunity.
5. Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV): Single dose for adults ≥ 75 years, or age 60-74 at increased risk.
6. COVID-19: Updated annual formulation targeting circulating dominant subvariants for all individuals ≥ 6 months.

=== 5. ACUTE GASTROENTERITIS & ORAL REHYDRATION PROTOCOL ===
Pathophysiology: Norovirus (most common cause of acute viral gastroenteritis in all ages), Rotavirus (pediatric), or bacterial (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, enterotoxigenic E. coli).

Clinical Assessment of Dehydration:
- Mild/Moderate: Dry oral mucosa, increased thirst, slightly sunken eyes, decreased skin turgor, dark concentrated urine.
- Severe: Extreme lethargy/unresponsiveness, absent tears, tachycardia with weak thready pulse, hypotension, anuria > 8 hours.

Oral Rehydration Therapy (WHO Standard ORS Formulation):
- Commercial ORS packets dissolved in 1 liter of clean potable water:
  * Sodium chloride: 2.6 g
  * Glucose anhydrous: 13.5 g
  * Potassium chloride: 1.5 g
  * Trisodium citrate dihydrate: 2.9 g
  (Osmolarity ~245 mOsm/L; optimal balance for intestinal sodium-glucose cotransporters).
- Homemade emergency solution: 6 level teaspoons sugar + 1/2 level teaspoon salt dissolved in 1 liter clean water.
- Administration: Frequent small sips (5–10 mL every 5 minutes) via spoon or cup. Avoid high-sugar carbonated beverages, sports drinks, or fruit juices alone (high osmolarity worsens secretory diarrhea).
- Red Flags Requiring Emergency Care: Bloody stools (dysentery), persistent high fever (> 38.5°C), intractable vomiting preventing oral fluid retention, severe rebound abdominal tenderness, confusion.
